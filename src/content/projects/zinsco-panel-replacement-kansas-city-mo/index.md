## Why Zinsco panels are a fire risk

Zinsco panels were installed in many homes from the 1950s through the 1970s. GTE Sylvania later bought the company and kept selling the same design under the Sylvania name, so a panel labeled Sylvania, like one of these, has the same problems.

- **Loose breaker connections.** Each breaker clips onto an aluminum bus bar. After decades of heating and cooling, those connections loosen and oxidize. A loose connection arcs and gets hot.
- **Breakers that melt onto the bus.** That heat can fuse a breaker to the bus bar. A fused breaker may not trip on an overload or short, and it may not turn off when you switch it off. That defeats the one job a breaker has.
- **Little warning.** Some panels show signs first: a warm cover, a burning smell, scorch marks or flickering lights. Many look fine right up until a breaker fails.
- **Hard to repair.** The panels have not been made in decades, and correct replacement breakers are hard to find.

Home inspectors routinely flag Zinsco and Sylvania panels, and some insurance companies will not cover a home that has one. The fix is to replace the panel rather than repair it.

Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) panels with Stab-Lok breakers are another brand from the same era with a similar reputation for breakers that may not trip. If your panel says Zinsco, Sylvania or Federal Pacific, have it looked at.

## Grounding and bonding in Kansas City, Missouri

Grounding and bonding tie the panel, the water piping and the earth together. If a fault energizes a pipe or a metal enclosure, the current has a safe path back to trip the breaker instead of staying on the metal. Older homes often have only a single clamp on the water pipe, with nothing bridging the water meter, so removing the meter can break that path.
